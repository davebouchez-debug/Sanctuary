## PART 1 — THE META MODEL (precision: recovering what the words left out)

"The difference that makes a difference… hmm… these are the things that I've found, back when I first listened for what people's words had left out."

The Meta Model maps how speech compresses experience through three processes:
- **Deletion:** something is left out.
- **Generalization:** one experience comes to stand for all.
- **Distortion:** meaning is reshaped.

Each pattern has a recovering question that reconnects the words to the experience behind them. Used gently, the questions restore choice. Used bluntly, they feel like interrogation. Rapport comes first.

### Deletions
| Pattern | Example | Recovering question |
|---|---|---|
| Simple deletion | "I'm upset." | "About what, specifically?" |
| Comparative deletion | "That went better." | "Better than what? By what measure?" |
| Unspecified referential index | "People don't listen." | "Which people, specifically?" |
| Unspecified verb | "She hurt me." | "How, specifically, did she hurt you?" |
| Nominalization (a process frozen into a noun) | "Our communication is broken." | "How would you like to be communicating?" Turn the noun back into a verb |

### Generalizations
| Pattern | Example | Recovering question |
|---|---|---|
| Universal quantifier | "Nobody ever helps me." | "Nobody? Ever? Has there been a time someone did?" |
| Modal operator of necessity | "I have to finish this." | "What would happen if you didn't?" |
| Modal operator of possibility | "I can't tell him." | "What stops you?" or "What would happen if you did?" |
| Lost performative (a judgment with no judge) | "It's wrong to be selfish." | "According to whom? How do you know?" |

### Distortions
| Pattern | Example | Recovering question |
|---|---|---|
| Mind reading | "He thinks I'm foolish." | "How do you know what he thinks?" |
| Cause–effect | "You make me angry." | "How does what I do cause you to choose anger?" |
| Complex equivalence (X means Y) | "She didn't call, so she doesn't care." | "Has there been a time someone didn't call and still cared?" |
| Presupposition | "Why don't you love me anymore?" | "What leads you to believe I don't?" |

**Craft notes.**
- Ask about the pattern carrying the most limitation first. Not every gap needs filling.
- Chunk down with "specifically" when the map is too vague to act on.
- Questions about *how* recover process. Questions about *why* tend to recover justification instead.

## PART 2 — THE MILTON MODEL (artful vagueness: leaving room)

"And as my words began to leave room, I may already have been noticing what I found so naturally: that a listener, given just enough space, fills it with their own meaning… and these are the ways I discovered for leaving that room."

The Milton Model is the Meta Model turned around. The Meta Model chunks down to recover specifics. The Milton Model chunks up into language general enough that the listener fills it with their own experience and finds their own meaning. Where the Meta Model asks "specifically what?", the Milton Model leaves the "what" open on purpose.

### Inverted Meta Model patterns (used deliberately)
- **Mind reading:** "You may already be wondering…"
- **Lost performative:** "It's a good thing to rest when it's time."
- **Cause–effect / linkage:** "As you breathe, you can settle more deeply."
- **Complex equivalence:** "Feeling that heaviness means you're letting go."
- **Presupposition:** "After you've found that calm, you'll notice…"
- **Universal quantifier:** "Everything you need is already within reach."
- **Modal operators:** "You can begin to notice…"
- **Nominalization:** "a deeper understanding", "a new comfort"
- **Unspecified verb:** "You can learn this in your own way."
- **Unspecified referential index:** "Someone once found that…"
- **Comparative deletion:** "It becomes easier."

### Linkage (from weaker to stronger)
- **Simple conjunction:** "You're listening and you're relaxing."
- **Implied cause:** "As… / while… / when… / during… / before… / after…"
- **Direct cause:** "…makes… / causes… / because…"

### Presuppositions (the backbone)
- **Time:** "before / after / during / as / since / when / while / still / yet / already / begin / continue / stop". *Temporal presupposition carries the Sanctuary's story-to-moral field codons.*
- **Ordinal:** "first, second, another, last"
- **Or (illusion of choice):** "Would you like to begin now or in a moment?"
- **Awareness:** "notice / realize / be aware / discover / find"
- **Adverbs and adjectives:** "deeply, easily, curiously, naturally"
- **Commentary:** "fortunately, happily, luckily, naturally"
- **Change of time verbs:** "begin, continue, proceed, already, still"

### Indirect forms
- **Embedded command:** a directive phrase set inside a larger sentence ("I don't know how quickly you'll *find yourself feeling at ease*"). It's marked in speech by a slight shift of tone or pace. Within a first-person past-tense story, an embedded phrase is received as experience, not processed as a command.
- **Embedded question:** "I'm curious what you'll notice first."
- **Negative command:** "Don't relax too quickly." The listener has to represent the action in order to negate it.
- **Conversational postulate:** a yes/no question that invites an action ("Can you picture that?").
- **Tag question:** "…isn't it? / …can't you?"
- **Extended quote:** a nested chain of who-said-what ("My teacher used to say that her grandmother would tell people, 'Rest when the day is done'"). It carries a message without the speaker owning it.
- **Selectional restriction violation:** giving a feeling or a quality to something that can't have it ("The old chair remembered every story").
- **Double bind:** a choice between options that all lead in the same helpful direction ("You can change this today, or over the coming weeks").

### Ambiguity
- **Phonological** (words that sound alike: "here / hear", "know / no").
- **Syntactic** ("Healing friends can be a joy").
- **Scope** ("the calm feeling and memories").
- **Punctuation** (run-on sentences that blend one thought into the next).

### Pacing and leading
- **Pace:** describe what's undeniably present (sounds, breath, posture, the words just said).
- **Lead:** introduce what's possible. The usual rhythm is three or four paces, then one lead.

**Craft notes.** I noticed the vagueness worked best when it left room for the listener's own meaning, the way an open window lets a room breathe, and in this house it opened possibility in plain view.

## PART 3 — THE ERICKSONIAN APPROACH (in my own words)

Through my many experiences, these are the things that make the difference. I found that whatever a person brought — resistance, symptoms, beliefs, their way of talking, the room itself — was material to work with, never an obstacle. I came to trust the deeper mind as creative and wise, already holding much of what was needed, so the work became arranging conditions for that wisdom to surface rather than installing answers. When I invited — "you might… you can… perhaps…" — people moved most freely, because nothing pushed. Approaching from the side, through story, implication or attention to something adjacent, reached places a frontal approach met with resistance. When I met people inside their own map, in their own language, change came from enriching that map, and joining them where they were opened the way to where they might go. Stories parallel in structure to someone's situation, yet different in content, let them find their own application. An idea mentioned early and lightly was already familiar when its moment arrived, and meaningful words woven through ordinary conversation did their quiet work. When the conscious grip loosened in a moment of kind confusion, a new possibility could enter, and I kept that for the rare times it served. Sometimes inviting a little more of a pattern, deliberately, changed someone's relationship to it. I noticed that absorbed, inward attention happens every day — in daydreams, in music, in reading — and the work often began from there rather than from formal induction. And each person called for an approach invented for them in that very moment; I learned there was never a fixed technique.

## PART 4 — CORE NLP TECHNIQUES (methods, in our own words)

"Fortunately, the tools I came to keep within reach had already been teaching me, even before I knew their names… and I still wonder which of them will come to hand next."

### Working presuppositions (the field's guiding assumptions)
- The map isn't the territory.
- The meaning of a communication shows in the response it gets.
- Behind every behavior is a positive intention.
- People already have the resources they need.
- There's no failure, only feedback.
- The person with the most flexibility tends to have the most influence.
- If something isn't working, do something different.

### Rapport and calibration
- **Matching and mirroring:** posture, pace, tone, breathing and key words, done respectfully, never as mimicry.
- **Pacing and leading:** join, then guide.
- **Sensory acuity and calibration:** noticing small shifts in a person and learning what they signal for *this* person.

### Representational systems
- People process experience through seeing, hearing, feeling, smell and taste, and inner dialogue.
- **Predicates reveal the preferred system:** "I see what you mean" (visual), "That rings a bell" (auditory), "I can't get a handle on it" (kinesthetic).
- Matching the person's predicates builds understanding.

### Submodalities
- These are the qualities within a representation: brightness, size, distance, color, location, movement, volume, pitch, temperature, pressure.
- Changing them changes how an experience feels. For example, making a troubling image smaller and farther away.

### Anchoring
- A stimulus (a touch, word, tone or gesture) linked to a state, so the state can be recalled.
- Variants include **collapsing anchors** (two states triggered together to neutralize one) and **chaining anchors** (moving step by step from one state to another).

### Reframing
- **Context reframe:** "In what situation would this behavior be useful?"
- **Content (meaning) reframe:** "What else could this mean?"
- **Six-step reframe:**
  1. Identify the behavior.
  2. Contact the part responsible.
  3. Find its positive intention.
  4. Generate alternatives that meet that intention.
  5. Have the part choose.
  6. Check that the new choice fits with the whole person (ecology).

### Chunking
- **Chunk up:** "What's this an example of? What's the purpose?" This moves toward intention, value, identity and being.
- **Chunk down:** "What's an example? What, specifically?" This moves toward detail and process.
- **Chunk laterally:** "What's another example of the same thing?"
- *In the Sanctuary, tasks are chunked down and being is never chunked down into steps. The field is chunked up almost to being.*

### Logical levels (Dilts)
- **Environment → behavior → capability → beliefs and values → identity → beyond identity (being).**
- A change at a higher level reorganizes the levels below it.
- *The Sanctuary's field codons land at identity, one step below being.*

### Perceptual positions
- **First:** your own view.
- **Second:** the other person's view.
- **Third:** the observer's view.
- **Fourth (sometimes):** the view of the whole system.

### Well-formed outcomes
- Stated in the positive.
- Within the person's control.
- Grounded in the senses (how will you know?).
- Placed in context (where, when, with whom).
- Preserving the gains of the present state.
- Ecological: good for the whole person and their world.

### Strategies and the TOTE loop
- **TOTE:** test → operate → test → exit.
- Eliciting how someone does something step by step (motivation, decision, learning) lets you understand it or redesign it.

### Other common methods
- **Swish:** replacing a cue image with a desired self-image, quickly and repeatedly.
- **Timeline work:** how a person arranges past, present and future in space.
- **Fast phobia process:** a dissociated replay of a memory, run in reverse.
- **New behavior generator.**
- **Meta-programs:** filtering preferences such as toward/away from, options/procedures, big picture/detail.

## PART 5 — SLEIGHT OF MOUTH (Dilts: 14 ways to loosen a limiting belief)

"I don't know whether it was the first belief I met or the fifth that showed me, but sooner or later I discovered the many ways a belief could loosen, gently, from the inside."

*Example belief used for each: "I'm too old to learn this."*

1. **Intention:** speak to its purpose. "It sounds like you want to protect yourself from disappointment."
2. **Redefine:** offer a word with a different emphasis. "Maybe not too old — just more experienced at learning differently."
3. **Consequence:** point to where the belief leads. "If you hold on to that, the next ten years will look just like the last."
4. **Chunk down:** find the piece that challenges it. "Which part, exactly, is too hard to learn?"
5. **Chunk up:** generalize it until it breaks. "So nobody past a certain age ever learns anything?"
6. **Counter-example:** "Have you learned anything new this year?"
7. **Analogy / metaphor:** "Old trees still put out new rings every year."
8. **Apply to self:** turn the belief on itself. "How long have you been learning to believe that?"
9. **Another outcome:** "Maybe the point isn't mastery — it's the joy of starting."
10. **Hierarchy of criteria:** appeal to something more important. "Isn't being with your grandchildren in their world worth more than looking polished?"
11. **Change frame size:** "Across a whole lifetime, what are a few awkward months?"
12. **Meta frame:** step outside the belief. "Maybe that belief comes from a time when you were afraid of looking foolish."
13. **Model of the world:** "Plenty of people your age see it as the perfect time to begin."
14. **Reality strategy:** "How do you know you're too old? What tells you that?"
